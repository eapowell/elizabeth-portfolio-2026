Rift Strike
Anatomy of a run

Ten minutes inside The Rift, pulled apart — the guest journey beat by beat, then the three mechanisms underneath it. Every frame on this page is real session capture.

Role
Product Lead & Board Director

Overview

Every shorthand people reach for is wrong.

It isn't VR — nothing goes on your head, and you walk through real rooms with real furniture. It isn't an escape room — there are no locks and you are never trapped. It isn't laser tag — you aren't shooting each other. The thing that makes Rift Strike worth building is exactly the thing that makes it hard to sell in a sentence.

The closest true sentence is this: the rooms are already full of things you can't see, and the device is the only way to see them. Everything below is how a guest gets to that realisation in under two minutes, and what has to be true underneath for it to land.

Act I — The run

A guest is on site for forty minutes. Ten of them are the round.

The other thirty are doing quiet, deliberate work: turning a group of individuals into a team, teaching four mechanics without breaking character, and solving a hard spatial-computing problem by asking someone to take a photograph. Twelve beats, in order.

FoyerPre-round

Name yourselves before you play

Two columns — an adjective and a noun — each with a filter box and a Roll. A party lands on Feathered Charms or Fake Cheetahs in about forty seconds of laughing at each other, and one person puts it up for the team.

Design note

This is the first thing a guest touches, and it is a naming toy rather than a form. Nobody has to invent a name from nothing, and the constraint is what makes it funny. By the time the round starts, a group of coworkers has become a team with a name they chose — which is most of the work of making a shared score matter.

BriefingPre-round

A person tells you the problem

A live-action agent of the Rift Investigation Society explains that the Rift has destabilised, that spiders are coming through, and that Thomas disappeared into it with Abigail. Then he explains the DART, and that it only works on creatures that aren’t fully anchored.

Design note

Rules delivered as exposition by a character on camera, not a stack of tooltips. Every mechanic a guest needs — what the weapon is, what it can hit, that there is a clock — arrives inside the fiction, and the missing-persons thread gives a ten-minute shooter a reason to exist.

SyncPre-round

Find the portrait. Fill the frame.

Three lines of instruction: find the matching portrait in the foyer, align it to fill the frame, your position will sync automatically. The group code — GEEK — sits underneath it.

Design note

The hardest technical problem in the product is handed to the guest as a photograph task, and they never learn it was hard. The group code is a pronounceable four-letter word rather than a QR code or a six-digit number, because it has to survive being shouted across a noisy lobby.

PracticePre-round

You recover the DART, you aren’t issued it

Five numbered steps, gated one at a time: recover it, equip it, toggle it on, shoot a training target above a painting, then lead a moving one. Each step waits until you actually do it.

Design note

Standard onboarding, rewritten so that no step breaks character. The final step — leading a moving target — exists because it is the single skill the round punishes most, and teaching it here is cheaper than losing a guest to frustration at minute four.

PracticePre-round

Ammunition is something you find in the room

You got 3 Fire Crystal. Crystals are scattered through the physical space and power the DART; their counter lives permanently in the bottom bar beside the weapon.

Design note

This is the mechanic that makes people walk. Nothing forces exploration in a maze you can see the end of — but a consumable found only by looking into corners turns the whole floorplan into playable surface, at zero cost in additional set dressing.

Round start00:00

The clock starts and the decor wakes up

Six live values arrive at once: time remaining, personal score, accuracy, team total, an objective chip and a hint button — plus the party roster, carrying photographs of the other players.

Design note

Six values and never a menu; the stack is readable at a glance while walking. The roster uses face photos rather than usernames, so when a teammate’s number jumps you know which body in the room did it.

Hunt00:54

It stays where it is when you look away

A spider holds position on a real wall. Walk toward it and it grows; step sideways and it holds. Its shadow falls on the actual paint.

Design note

Persistence is the entire illusion. A creature that tracked the camera would read as a sticker on the screen; one that stays on the wall reads as a thing in the building. Everything else in this case study is in service of this one moment landing.

Hunt03:32

Sparks land in the room, not on the glass

Impacts resolve at the creature’s position in world space, with the DART barrel and muzzle glow rendered along the bottom-right edge of frame in the same brass as the physical props.

Design note

The weapon art is anchored to the device rather than centred like a console shooter, so the guest’s actual hand and the rendered barrel occupy the same corner of vision. It is a small thing, and it is what stops the device reading as a screen you are holding up.

Gate~05:00

500 team points lures out the Boss

New objective unlocked. Reach 500 Team points to lure out the Boss Spider. Every spider you eliminate gets your team closer.

Design note

The gate reads the team total, not yours. A strong player cannot unlock the boss alone and a weak one cannot lock it out, so the fast player’s incentive is to keep the slow one shooting. One threshold does the work that cooperative design usually needs a whole mechanic for.

Boss06:12

It comes through a doorway you just walked through

The Boss Spider fills the frame, legs past both edges, backlit orange. A Goal completed toast slides in underneath it.

Design note

Scale is the payoff for a round spent shooting things the size of a hand. Because it spawns in a corridor the party has already cleared, the space they thought was safe is the space it arrives in — the reveal costs a doorway, not a set.

Debrief10:00

The number you argue about in the car

Team total up top, then a card per player: score, MVP badge, accuracy, hits over shots, a per-creature kill breakdown, and two verdicts — boss unlocked, boss killed.

Design note

Separating unlocked from killed is the sharpest call in the scoring. It gives a team that got close a real result to be annoyed about rather than a flat loss, and it names precisely what a second round would buy them.

Front deskPost

Spend it on something you can eat

Rift Dove Chocolate Treat — purchasing this item allows you to pick one up from a RIS Agent at the front desk. Limit one per explorer. Seventy-five points.

Design note

The in-game economy settles in the real building. Points earned shooting spiders are redeemed from a staff member who is in character as an Agent, which closes the loop the sync step opened: the digital layer reaches back out and puts an object in your hand.

Act II — The machine

Three mechanisms carry the whole illusion.

Each one is a place where the digital layer and the physical building have to agree with each other. Break any of them and the product collapses back into a phone game people happen to be playing in a themed room.

Fallen Orreries sprayed — 207 shots at 58% to reach 709. Fake Cheetahs were surgical — 138 shots, 78% on the better gun — and landed on 706. The scoring is doing what it should: it rewards two completely different ways of playing, and neither team can tell from the number alone that they played nothing alike.

The split is at the bottom of the card. Both teams cleared the 500-point gate and pulled the Boss Spider out. Only the team that had been firing constantly still had the volume to put it down before the clock ran out.

The gate is generous. The boss is not. That difference is the most reliable reason a group walks back to the desk and buys another round.

Where it landed

A second experience, shipped and selling.

Rift Strike was built as the shorter, faster counterpart to Dragon Capture — aimed at kids, families and returning players who wanted a quicker way back in.

Reflection

The best decisions here look like nothing.

Nobody walks out of The Rift talking about image-target localization. They talk about the spider that was still on the wall when they looked back. The work of this product is choosing, over and over, to spend engineering on the thing a guest will feel and never name — and to hand them the hard parts as something easy, like taking a photograph of a portrait.

The other lesson is about where a design ends. I used to think the product stopped at the edge of the screen. It doesn’t: it stops at the front desk, where a staff member in character hands over a chocolate someone earned by shooting spiders. Designing that hand-off — and the moment an Agent sells you another round without breaking the story — turned out to matter as much as anything inside the app.
